Three Phases of a Hunt

Strategy Overview: Every successful foxhunt follows three phases based on your distance from the fox. Recognising which phase you are in determines your technique.
Phase 1 · Long Range

Long Yagi Antenna

Use full gain. Pan slowly to find the strongest signal direction. Walk decisively toward the bearing.

Phase 2 · Mid Range

Attenuator + Loop

Signal is strong — add attenuation. Switch to a directional loop to refine the bearing.

Phase 3 · Close Range

Body Shielding

Use your body as a shield. The null zone points toward the fox. Switch to a rubber duck antenna.

Body Shielding Technique

How it works: Your body absorbs 20–30 dB of signal. When signal is weakest (null), the fox is behind you. Rotate slowly to find the null.
⚡ Why is Body Shielding Phase 3?
Each phase solves the specific problem of that distance. Far away you need more sensitivity — a Yagi at full gain. Getting closer you need more precision — a loop with an attenuator. Right next to the fox the signal is so strong it saturates your receiver — the S-meter reads full scale in every direction and a Yagi becomes useless. Body shielding is the only technique that handles signal overload while still giving you a direction.
📶 Signal Overload — Full Progression
Distance Problem Solution
> 300 m Signal too weak to detect Yagi — full gain
100 – 300 m Signal getting strong Attenuator — reduce receiver gain
30 – 100 m Signal very strong Attenuator + Body shield
< 30 m Receiver saturated — S-meter maxed everywhere Body shield only (attenuator maxed out)
💡 Think of it as a volume dial: Yagi turns it up → Attenuator turns it down → Body shield covers your ears.
💧
Your Body = RF Absorber
The human body is ~60% water. Water absorbs VHF radio waves (144 MHz) very well, blocking 20–30 dB — that means the signal behind you is 100× to 1000× weaker.
🔄
Why Rotate Your Whole Body
If you only rotate the antenna, the shield (your body) does not move. The fox must end up directly behind your back — your torso is the "wall" blocking the signal.
🎯
Reading the Null
Turn until the S-meter reads minimum (null). The fox is now directly at your back. Add 180° to your facing direction → that is your fox bearing. Works best within 100 m.
🔁 Why Switch Antenna? Yagi vs Rubber Duck
▶ Step-by-Step
  1. Switch to a short rubber-duck antenna (long Yagi reduces body-shield effect).
  2. Hold the antenna vertically in front of your chest at waist height.
  3. Rotate your whole body slowly in a full circle. Watch the S-meter drop.
  4. Stop at the deepest null (lowest reading). The fox is directly behind you.
  5. Note your facing compass heading, then add 180° — that is the fox bearing. Walk toward it.

Plan Your Route

Study the map before the start. Note terrain features that could block signals.

Mark Bearings

Plot each bearing on your map with a pencil. The intersection narrows the search area.

When Signals Conflict

Reflections off buildings can mislead. Take multiple bearings from different spots before committing.

Manage Your Time

Spend no more than 20 minutes on one fox. Move on and come back — other foxes may be nearby.

Try Both Polarities

Rotate antenna between vertical and horizontal — some foxes transmit in a different polarisation.

Use Terrain

Hills and ridgelines can block signal and create false nulls. Walk around obstacles.

Record Your Hunt

After each event, review what worked. Champions keep notes and improve every hunt.

Team Sport

Even solo events benefit from post-hunt debriefs. Compare routes with other hunters.

IARU ARDF Competition Rules

📹 Fox Transmission Cycle — 5-Minute Rotation
Gold bar = transmitting • Each fox waits 4 minutes between its 1-minute broadcast
0:00 / 5:00 simulated cycle
  • Standard ARDF events use 5 foxes on the 2-metre band (144 MHz).
  • Each fox transmits in sequence: 1 minute on, 4 minutes silent (rotation period: 5 minutes).
  • Competitors must find as many foxes as possible within the time limit (typically 80–120 minutes).
  • Each fox has a physical punch; competitors record the punch on their card.
  • Score = points for foxes found (value depends on order found) minus time penalty.
  • School ARDF events often use simplified scoring: points per fox minus elapsed minutes.

Propagation Effects in ARDF

📹 Signal Propagation Scenarios — tap to explore
Line of sight: VHF signals travel in straight lines. Your receiver gets a clear, accurate bearing toward the fox.
  • VHF signals (144 MHz) travel mostly in straight lines — obstructions cause shadows.
  • Reflections from buildings, hills and metallic structures can produce false bearings.
  • Ground-wave propagation dominates at short range; sky-wave is negligible at 144 MHz over short distances.
  • Dense vegetation attenuates signals — 10–20 dB extra loss in heavy forest.
  • Signal fading occurs when you move between reflection zones — look for the moving null.

Advanced Direction-Finding Techniques

📹 Antenna Radiation Pattern — select type
Loop antenna
Cardioid
Yagi beam
Null direction
Loop antenna: produces a figure-8 pattern with two sharp nulls (minimum signal). Rotate until the signal disappears — the null points toward the fox.
  • Sense antenna: A small sense element eliminates the 180° ambiguity of a loop antenna.
  • Cardioid pattern: Combining a loop and sense element produces a heart-shaped pattern with one null.
  • 5-element Yagi: Provides 9–10 dBd gain and sharp front-to-back ratio for long-range phases.
  • Stepped attenuator: 10 dB steps; use minimum attenuation needed to prevent S-meter saturation.
  • Auditory null: At very close range, use headphones for a sharper null than the S-meter can provide.
