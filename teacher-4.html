📋 Final Module Overview

Module 4 is the capstone module. Scouts apply all learning from Modules 0–3 in a live competitive hunt. The key pedagogical goals are phase-based thinking, signal interpretation, and systematic field navigation.

✅ T1: Fill-in-Blanks — Answer Key

Q1
Phase 1 uses the ___ antenna
Yagi — the directional 3-element Yagi gives 6–8 dBi gain over a dipole, essential for long-range bearings. Accept: "3-element Yagi", "directional antenna".
Q2
Body shielding uses the ___ of your body
body (accept: absorption, mass). The human body (70% water) effectively absorbs 144 MHz VHF signals, attenuating signal by 15–25 dB behind the hunter.
Q3
When signal is very strong, switch to a ___ antenna
rubber duck (accept: "small antenna", "whip", "short antenna"). The rubber duck/helical antenna has much lower gain (~0 dBi vs Yagi's 7 dBi), reducing overload.
Q4
The S-meter measures signal ___
strength. The S-meter (Signal meter) displays received signal strength in S-units. S9 = −73 dBm; each S-unit = 6 dB.
Q5
Log the time and ___ for each fox found
location (accept: bearing, grid reference, coordinate, landmark). Both time and location are required on the official fox log sheet.
Q6
The ___ technique uses body rotation
body shielding (accept: "body shield"). The formal ARDF term is body shielding or "body as attenuator". The mnemonic "Fox Dance" is informal but widely used.
Q7
Competition time limit is ___ minutes
80 minutes — this is the IARU Region 3 standard for 144 MHz ARDF. Note: junior events may use 60 minutes locally.

🐛 T2: Debug Scenarios — Model Answers

Debug #1 — Phase Transition Failure

Model Answer
The mistake: The hunter is stuck in Phase 1 behaviour (chasing the strongest signal) even at close range. At close range, VHF signals reflect off terrain and foliage, so the "strongest" direction is unreliable — it can lead to circles or false paths.

Correct approach: When signal reaches S7+ (roughly under 100 m), transition to Phase 2 by adding an attenuator and reducing gain. Under 30 m (S9+), switch to rubber duck only, and use body shielding. Rotate 360°, find the null (weakest) direction, flip 180° — that is the fox bearing. Walk 5–10 m and repeat.

Debug #2 — Signal Disappears (3 causes)

Model Answer (accept any 3)
1. Beacon cycle timing: The hunter is listening during the MO rest period between fox transmissions. Fix: wait 12–60 seconds for the cycle to repeat.

2. Frequency drift or wrong channel: Radio accidentally bumped to wrong frequency. Fix: verify 144.500 MHz (or local beacon frequency) and rescan.

3. Terrain shadowing (RF shadow): A hill or dense stand of trees is directly between the hunter and fox. Fix: move laterally 20–30 m to break the shadow, then listen again.

4. Low battery: Radio or antenna lost power. Fix: check/replace batteries; carry spares.

5. Too close — overload squelch: The receiver is so close the signal overloads and the squelch cuts the audio. Fix: reduce squelch, switch to rubber duck, use maximum attenuation.

Debug #3 — Scoring Calculation (Worked Example)

Hunt start: 09:00 | Time limit: 80 min | Deadline: 10:20

Formula: score_per_fox = 80 − elapsed_minutes_at_find

MO5 found at 10:00 → elapsed = 60 min → score = 80 − 60 = 20 pts
MOE found at 10:10 → elapsed = 70 min → score = 80 − 70 = 10 pts

Check-in at 10:20 → elapsed = 80 min → ON TIME → no penalty

Total score = 20 + 10 = 30 points
Note: if check-in was late (e.g. 10:25 = 5 min late), deduct 5 pts → total 25 pts.
Marking Note

Award marks for correct formula application per fox. Scouts who get each individual score right but add incorrectly should receive partial credit. The worked example in the student page shows the formula clearly — look for understanding of the concept over arithmetic accuracy.

📡 Phase Transition Timing Advice

Scouts consistently struggle with when to transition phases. Use this table in your pre-hunt briefing:

CueWhat it meansAction
Signal first heard, very weak (S1–S2)Long range, Phase 1Take compass bearing, plot on map, start walking
Signal reaches S5–S6Closing in, ~200 mAdd −10 dB attenuator, continue Phase 2 tracking
Signal overloads even with attenuatorVery close, ~30–50 mRemove Yagi, use rubber duck only, begin body shielding
Null clearly found (near silence in one direction)Fox is directly behind youFlip 180°, walk 10 m toward fox, punch card
⚠️ Common Timing Mistake

Scouts delay Phase 3 too long — they keep the Yagi even when standing 5 m from the fox, then get confused by reflections. Emphasise: if you can hear the fox without an antenna plugged in, you are in Phase 3.

⚠️ Common Hunt Mistakes

Mistake 1: Not Switching Phases

Teams that use only Phase 1 technique for the entire hunt tend to orbit the fox in a spiral, never converging. This wastes 15–20 minutes. Facilitator action: Before the hunt, drill the transition points with a tabletop exercise using the S-meter widget.

Mistake 2: Ignoring Body Shielding

Scouts trained only in Yagi techniques have no close-range strategy. They walk past the fox at 5 m and keep going. Facilitator action: Run a 10-minute indoor body-shield demo with a test transmitter before the hunt — let scouts feel the null with their own body.

Mistake 3: Chasing the Strongest Signal

Reflections from hills and large metallic objects (vehicles, buildings) can create false peaks. Teams that follow maximum signal instead of taking cross-bearings get badly misled. Facilitator action: Emphasise: take bearings from TWO different positions before moving; the triangulation point is more reliable than any single peak reading.

Mistake 4: Poor Time Management

Juniors especially spend too long on a single fox, missing others. Reinforce the 15-minute rule: if you haven't found a fox in 15 minutes of close searching, mark it on the map and move to the next. You can return if time allows.

🦺 Field Safety Checklist

Verify Before Hunt Start
  • All scouts wearing closed-toe shoes — check at start line, send back anyone non-compliant
  • Each team has: water (minimum 500 mL per person), emergency whistle, first aid card with facilitator phone number
  • Walk the boundary markers with all teams before the hunt starts
  • Emergency procedure briefed: 3 whistle blasts = emergency, stay put, do not continue hunting
  • At least two facilitators / marshals with radios on the course at all times
  • All mobile phones confirmed OFF (or confiscated) before Start horn
  • First aid kit at Start/Finish with qualified person present

💬 Facilitator Debrief Questions

Use these in the post-hunt debrief session (14:00–14:30). Encourage scouts to answer from their own experience, not from the theory.

Q1
At what point during the hunt did you realise you needed to change technique?
Listen for: phase transition moments, S-meter overload, body shield discovery. Bridges theory to lived experience.
Q2
What was the most surprising thing the radio signal did in the field?
Elicits reflection on multipath, terrain effects, signal anomalies — builds intuition for RF propagation.
Q3
How did your team make decisions under time pressure?
Leadership, communication, prioritisation. Bridges to real-world STEM career skills.
Q4
If you could place the beacons for the next group, where would you hide them and why?
Reverses perspective — scouts must think like the puzzle-setter. Reveals understanding of signal propagation and terrain.
Q5
How is systematic fox hunting like good engineering? What parallels do you see?
Connects the workshop to STEM learning: hypothesis-test cycles, systematic elimination, tool selection, data logging.
Q6
What one thing would you teach someone who has never heard of ARDF?
Consolidates learning through the protégé effect. Excellent for journal/portfolio evidence. Wrap-up question.
