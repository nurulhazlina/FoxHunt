What Is Morse Code?

Imagine texting someone using only two buttons — a short press and a long press. That’s Morse code! Every letter is a unique combination of short beeps (dits ·) and long beeps (dahs —).

In Radio Foxhunting, the hidden transmitters 🦊 broadcast their ID in Morse code. Your job: listen, count the beeps, and know which fox you are chasing!

The 60-Second Beacon Cycle

Real-world scenario 🌿

You’re in the forest. Your radio picks up: dah-dah · dah-dah-dah · dit · dit — that’s MOI (Fox 2)! You have about 8 seconds left in its slot. Quickly point your antenna to find its direction, mark it on your map, then wait 48 seconds for its next turn.

⌛ Hunter’s strategy: The order never changes (MOE → MOI → MOS → MOH → MO5). Once you know where you are in the cycle, predict which fox comes next and be ready before it transmits!

Did You Know? 🌍

🚢
Titanic used Morse Code
In 1912, the Titanic’s radio operators sent SOS as the ship sank. It was one of the first real uses of the SOS signal — and it helped rescue 710 survivors.
🌐
Still used today!
Amateur radio operators still use Morse code worldwide. It works better than voice when signals are very weak — a faint dit-dah gets through when voice is just noise.
🏃
ARDF is a world sport
Radio Foxhunting (ARDF) is competitive in over 40 countries. World championships are held every two years, combining orienteering, radio skills and strategic thinking.
🇲🇾
Malaysian RAE includes Morse
The Malaysian RAE covers Morse timing ratios (1:3:7). Even though speed sending is no longer required for a licence, the theory is still tested in the exam!
⚡
Speed record: 216 chars/min
Expert Morse operators can send over 200 characters per minute — around 3–4 letters every second! Beginners start at 5 WPM; 12 WPM is comfortable for most.
📡
80-metre band for ARDF
Classic ARDF uses the 80 m amateur band (3.5 MHz). Signals bend around hills and buildings — perfect for hiding foxes in tricky terrain like a jungle or urban area!

What You’ll Hear in the Field FIELD GUIDE

A step-by-step guide to listening at a foxhunt start area — from first beep to bearing noted.

1
Wait for the 700 Hz tone
All ARDF beacons transmit on the same frequency. Tune your receiver and wait — the tone starts when a beacon’s 12-second slot begins. You’ll hear a clear continuous note before the Morse.
2
Let the “MO” prefix pass
Every beacon starts with MO (— — — · — — —). This is the common prefix — ignore it. Listen for the pause after; that’s where the identity dits begin.
3
Count the short dits after “MO”
After MO, you’ll hear 1–5 short “dit” sounds. That count IS the fox number: 1 dit = Fox 1 (MOE), 2 dits = Fox 2 (MOI), and so on up to 5 (MO5). Count out loud to be sure.
4
Note your compass bearing
While the beacon is transmitting, rotate your directional antenna to find the signal null (direction of minimum signal). Read your compass. That bearing points toward the fox — write it down.
5
Wait and confirm in the next cycle
Each fox transmits for 12 seconds every 60 seconds. After your fox’s slot ends, the other four transmit in sequence. Your fox returns within 60 seconds — listen again to confirm your bearing before you move.
💡 Pro tip: new participants often count MO’s dits instead of the identity dits. Practice counting just the dits that come after the long pause following MO.

Prosigns RAE

Prosigns (procedural signs) are special Morse sequences sent as a single run — no letter spacing between characters. They are procedural shorthand used to control a contact.

RAE Exam Corner RAE

Module 1 covers several topics that appear in the Malaysian RAE paper. Here’s what to know cold before exam day.

Timing Ratios (memorise these)

  • ★Dit : Dah = 1 : 3 — a dash is exactly three times a dot in duration.
  • ★Intra-character gap = 1 unit (same as a dit).
  • ★Inter-character gap = 3 units (same as a dah).
  • ★Word gap = 7 units — the longest silence.

ARDF / Foxhunt Facts

  • ✔Standard ARDF band: 3.5 MHz (80-metre amateur band).
  • ✔Beacon cycle: 60 seconds total, 12 seconds per fox, 5 foxes in sequence.
  • ✔Beacon order: MOE → MOI → MOS → MOH → MO5 (1 to 5 dits).
  • ✔Typical tone: 700 Hz audio note on CW (Continuous Wave).
  • ✔SOS has no letter spacing — one prosign run: ··· −−− ···

Useful Q-Codes for ARDF

Q-codes are three-letter shorthand used in CW contacts and radio procedure. These appear in both the RAE paper and real foxhunts.

QRM
Man-made interference — another station is causing static on your frequency.
QRN
Atmospheric / natural static — lightning crashes, weather noise.
QSY
Change frequency — “Shall we move to another frequency?”
QTH
Location — “What is your location?” / “My location is …”
QSO
A radio contact — “Can you communicate with …?”
QRZ
“Who is calling me?” — used when you hear a station but can’t identify it.
📋 RAE Note: Q-codes are asked in the RAE both as definitions and in context. Know them as concepts, not just labels.
