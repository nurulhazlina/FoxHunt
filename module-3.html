📻 How Radio Works — Why Antennas Matter

Radio is invisible — but it is real! When you press the button on a walkie-talkie, electricity inside the radio creates radio waves that fly through the air at the speed of light. These waves reach another radio's antenna, turn back into electricity, and you hear a voice. The antenna is the key link — it is the bridge between electricity and the open air.

Radio waves travel from transmitter → through the air → to your antenna → into your receiver

⚡
Transmitter (TX) A walkie-talkie or foxhunt beacon that turns sound or data into radio waves and sends them out via its antenna.
〰️
Radio Waves Invisible energy waves travelling at 300,000 km/s. They pass through walls, trees and the atmosphere — no wires needed!
📡
Antenna A carefully sized piece of metal that catches radio waves and converts them back to tiny electrical signals. Shape and size set which frequency it captures best.
🎧
Receiver (RX) A radio that amplifies the tiny antenna signal, decodes it, and turns it into sound — voice, beeps, or Morse code.
🌊 Frequency & Wavelength: 144 MHz means 144 million wave cycles every second. At this speed, one full wave measures about 2.08 metres. Antenna length is directly tied to wavelength — that is why each element in your Yagi is an exact fraction of 2.08 m.

📡 Types of Antennas — From Simple to Directional

Not all antennas look or work the same. Some send and receive equally in all directions (omnidirectional) — like a torch with no reflector. Others focus energy in one direction (directional) — like a spotlight. For foxhunting we need a directional antenna so we can tell WHERE the hidden fox is.

💡 Key Insight: An omnidirectional antenna hears the fox but cannot tell you the DIRECTION. A Yagi points at the fox — rotate until signal is strongest and you have your bearing!

📡 What is a Yagi Antenna?

A Yagi–Uda antenna (commonly called a Yagi) is a directional antenna that focuses radio energy in one direction — like a torch beam. For ARDF (Amateur Radio Direction Finding), this directional property lets you determine which direction the hidden transmitter (fox) is located.

🎯
Directional Gain Amplifies signals from the front, making distant foxes easier to hear than with a simple whip antenna.
↔️
Front-to-Back Ratio How much stronger the front signal is vs the back. A 3-element Yagi achieves ~20 dB — 100× more signal from the front!
🔕
Side Nulls Very little signal arrives from the sides. Rotating to find the null precisely pins down the fox's bearing.
📐
Beamwidth The angular spread of the main lobe is ~60–80° for a 3-element Yagi — narrow enough for direction finding.

Why Yagi for ARDF?

The tape-measure Yagi is the foxhunter's weapon of choice: elements fold flat for transport, the build costs less than a meal, and the gain is enough to hear the fox's 5-watt signal from over a kilometre away in dense jungle. Add its sharp nulls for precise bearings and it outperforms every rubber-duck antenna in the field.

ARDF Standard: The 2 metre ARDF band is 144.000–146.000 MHz. Foxes transmit at ~144.500 MHz in most events. Our Yagi is optimised for 144 MHz.

🔧 Building Steps

Follow these 8 steps to build the tape-measure Yagi. Work in pairs — one holds, one fastens. Estimated build time: 45–60 minutes.

1

Cut Tape Segments

Cut three strips: 104.0 cm (Reflector), 99.4 cm (Driven), 94.6 cm (Director). Mark each with nail polish for identification.

2

Mark & Drill PVC Boom

On 120 cm of ½″ PVC pipe, mark positions: Reflector at 0 cm, Driven at 36.5 cm, Director at 67.0 cm. Drill 4 mm holes at each mark.

3

Attach Reflector

Thread a self-tapping screw through the centre of the 104 cm tape segment into the boom at 0 cm. Ensure it is perpendicular and centred.

4

Mount Driven Element

Fit the SO-239 chassis connector on a small PVC saddle at 36.5 cm. Connect each half of the 99.4 cm element to the connector terminals.

5

Attach Director

Fasten the 94.6 cm tape segment at 67.0 cm from the reflector end. Ensure it is parallel and level with the other elements.

6

Solder Coax

Prepare 50-ohm RG-58 coax. Solder the centre conductor to the SO-239 centre pin and the shield braid to the outer ground terminal.

7

Check SWR

Connect an SWR meter, transmit briefly at low power (5 W max). Target SWR ≤ 1.5:1 at 144 MHz. Adjust driven element ±1 cm if needed.

8

Wrap & Secure

Wrap coax connections with self-amalgamating tape. Secure the coax along the boom with cable ties. Test fold/unfold of tape elements.

⚠️ Safety: Wear safety glasses when cutting tape measure — the coiled spring can snap and cause eye injury. An adult must supervise soldering steps.

🦊 Using the Yagi for ARDF

Building the antenna is only half the skill — using it correctly in the field is what wins the foxhunt.

Slow Rotation Technique

  1. Hold the Yagi horizontal, at waist height, boom parallel to the ground.
  2. Rotate slowly through 360° while listening to the signal strength.
  3. Find the direction of peak signal (front lobe pointing at fox).
  4. Note the compass bearing on your map.
  5. Confirm by rotating to the side null (~90° from front) for a sharper bearing.

Advanced Techniques

  • Body shielding: When very close, turn your back to the fox and use your body to attenuate the signal — use the null behind the Yagi for precision.
  • Close-in technique: Within ~50 m, detach the coax and use a rubber duck — the Yagi overloads the receiver at close range.
  • Sense antenna: A small omnidirectional element resolves front/back ambiguity in challenging terrain.
⚡ Common Trap: When the signal is strong in ALL directions, you are very close to the fox (within 50 m). Switch to your rubber duck or a simple dipole for the final approach — the Yagi's gain becomes a liability at close range.

💻 Reference Code

// Element length calculator for Yagi antennas
// Based on: λ/2 × velocity_factor

function elementLength(freqMHz, velocityFactor, element) {
  var halfWaveCm = (14300 / freqMHz) * velocityFactor;
  switch(element) {
    case 'reflector': return (halfWaveCm * 1.05).toFixed(1); // ~5% longer
    case 'driven':    return (halfWaveCm * 0.97).toFixed(1); // ~3% shorter
    case 'director':  return (halfWaveCm * 0.90).toFixed(1); // ~10% shorter
    default: return halfWaveCm.toFixed(1);
  }
}

// For 144 MHz, tape measure vf ≈ 0.97:
console.log('Reflector:', elementLength(144, 0.97, 'reflector'), 'cm'); // → 104.0 cm
console.log('Driven:   ', elementLength(144, 0.97, 'driven'),    'cm'); // → 96.4 cm (fine-tune to 99.4)
console.log('Director: ', elementLength(144, 0.97, 'director'),  'cm'); // → 89.8 cm (fine-tune to 94.6)

📡 Radiation Patterns & Gain

An omnidirectional antenna radiates equally in all horizontal directions. A directional antenna (Yagi) focuses energy like a spotlight. Gain is the ratio of focused power versus a reference antenna.

📊 dBd vs dBi

dBi = gain vs theoretical isotropic point source. dBd = gain vs half-wave dipole. Formula: dBi = dBd + 2.15. Every 3 dB doubles effective radiated power.

Rubber duck: 2 dBi = −0.15 dBd 3-el Yagi: 10 dBi = 7.85 dBd 5-el Yagi: 13 dBi = 10.85 dBd
📐 Beamwidth & Directivity

Beamwidth is the angle between the −3 dB points on the radiation pattern. A 3-element 144 MHz Yagi has a ~70° horizontal beamwidth. Narrower beam = more gain = harder to aim — key trade-off for foxhunting.

↕️ Polarization

Horizontal elements → horizontal polarization. Fox must match! Cross-polarization penalty ≈ 20 dB loss.

↔️ Front-to-Back Ratio

3-el Yagi ≈ 20 dB F/B ratio — front is 100× stronger than rear. Use rear null to confirm bearing.

📶 What is SWR?

Standing Wave Ratio measures how well the antenna impedance matches the feedline. A mismatch causes power to reflect back toward the transmitter.

1:1
Perfect ✓
1.5:1
~4% loss
2:1
~11% loss
3:1
~25% loss
⚡ Impedance

Standard coax is 50 Ω. When antenna impedance differs, power reflects back and SWR rises.

Half-wave dipole: 73 Ω Folded dipole: 288 Ω 50 Ω coax (standard) 3-el Yagi driven: ~25–50 Ω
🔧 Matching Techniques
Gamma match — adjustable shorting rod alongside driven element; slide until SWR meter reads minimum.
Beta / hairpin match — a short shorted wire across the feed point; changes impedance without moving parts.
Balun (4:1) — transforms 288 Ω folded dipole to 72 Ω; also converts balanced antenna to unbalanced coax.
💡 Tape-measure Yagi tip: The simple dipole driven element gives ~25–50 Ω. Shorten the driven element slightly until SWR < 1.5:1 on a meter or NanoVNA — no matching network needed.
🔢 Wavelength Formula
λ (m) = 300 / f (MHz)
144 MHz → λ = 300 ÷ 144 = 2.083 m
