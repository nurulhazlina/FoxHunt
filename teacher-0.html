Module 0 of 5 — Teacher Guide

Orientation — Facilitator Notes

Answer keys, teaching notes, common confusions, and Google Sheets submission schema for Module 0.

📋 Workshop Overview

Module 0 is the onboarding module. Scouts arrive with no assumed ARDF knowledge. By the end, every scout should understand the competition structure, have collected and identified their equipment, and have registered their team profile.

Duration
30 minutes (orientation) + 20 min activities
Age Groups
Junior (12–14), Senior (15–17), Open (18+)
Team Size
2–4 scouts per team recommended
Prerequisite
None — open to all scouts

🎯 Orientation Learning Goals

By the end of Module 0 every scout should be able to:

  • Define ARDF and explain how it differs from regular orienteering
  • Identify the 5 beacons (MOE–MO5) and the MO rest beacon by their Morse patterns
  • Identify each piece of equipment by name and purpose
  • Explain why 144 MHz equipment is used (not CB radio or phone GPS)
  • Register a team profile and understand the scoring/time-penalty rules

✅ T1: Fill-in-Blanks — Answer Key

Q1
ARDF stands for blank Radio blank Finding.
Amateur Radio Direction Finding
Accept: "amateur" / "AMATEUR" / any capitalisation. "Direction" must be exact — accept "directional" with note.
Q2
Foxhunting combines orienteering with ___
radio (or "radio direction finding", "ARDF", "electronics"). Do not accept "GPS" or "phone".
Q3
There are ___ hidden foxes in standard ARDF.
5 — this is the ITU/IARU standard. Note: some local events use 3 foxes for beginners, but the standard answer here is 5.
Q4
The hunting time limit is ___ minutes.
80 minutes. This is the standard for 144 MHz ARDF events per IARU Region 3 rules.
Q5
Each beacon transmits for ___ seconds per cycle.
12 seconds. The full cycle is 60 seconds: 5 foxes × 12 s = 60 s. The MO rest beacon fills remaining time.
Q6
The rest beacon callsign is ___
MO — accept "MO beacon". The MO transmission signals the start/end of each cycle. It is also the Start/Finish beacon.
Q7
You navigate using a ___ and topographic ___
compass and topographic map. Accept "baseplate compass" and "topo map". Do NOT accept GPS, phone, or app — these are prohibited in official ARDF.

🐛 T2: Debug Scenarios — Model Answers

Debug #1 — Wrong Navigation Tool

Model Answer
Problem: Mobile phone GPS relies on satellite positioning and network data — it drains battery quickly, may lose signal under tree cover, and (most importantly) is prohibited in ARDF competitions as it bypasses the navigation challenge.

Fix: Use a baseplate compass to take magnetic bearings to the signal source, and a 1:10,000 topographic map to plot position and plan routes. These skills are the core of ARDF — the challenge is combining radio direction-finding with traditional land navigation.

Debug #2 — Wrong Radio Band

Model Answer
Problem: A 27 MHz CB radio is designed to receive signals in the 26–28 MHz range. ARDF beacons transmit on 144 MHz (2-metre amateur band). The two bands are completely different — the CB radio cannot receive the beacon at all.

Fix: Use a 144 MHz handheld amateur radio receiver (e.g. Yaesu FT-60, Baofeng UV-5R) paired with a 3-element Yagi antenna tuned for 144 MHz.

Debug #3 — Time Penalty Calculation

Model Answer
Analysis: Time limit = 80 min. Elapsed at finish = 95 min. Late by 15 minutes.

Standard penalty rule: 1 point deducted per full minute late (varies by event — confirm with your competition rules). Some events use: score = (80 − elapsed_minutes) per fox if returned on time; if late, each late minute subtracts points from total.

Simplified scoring: 3 foxes found. Using formula: 1 point per fox, −1 per minute late → Score = 3 − 15 = −12 (disqualified in most events; they score 0).
Note: in IARU rules, teams finishing more than 5 minutes late are typically classified outside the result list.

⚠️ Common Scout Confusions

Confusion 1: CB Radio vs VHF (144 MHz)

"My dad has a CB radio at home — can I use that?" — Very common question, especially from older scouts.

Explain: CB radio operates at 27 MHz (HF band). ARDF beacons transmit at 144 MHz (VHF band). The antenna size, receiver circuitry and propagation characteristics are completely different. A CB radio simply cannot receive a 144 MHz signal — it's the wrong radio for the wrong frequency. Use: Baofeng UV-5R, Yaesu FT-60, or any VHF/UHF amateur handheld.

Confusion 2: GPS / Phone Navigation vs Compass + Map

Scouts often assume a phone is "better" than a compass. This misunderstands both the sport and the rules.

Explain: (1) ARDF rules prohibit electronic positioning devices — using one = disqualification. (2) The sport deliberately develops compass and map skills. (3) Phones may lose signal under dense canopy. (4) A baseplate compass + topo map is faster when you know how to use them.

Confusion 3: "Just follow the loudest signal"

Explain: VHF signals reflect off trees and hills — the loudest direction is not always the direct path to the fox. Scouts need to triangulate using multiple bearings from different positions, and switch to body-shielding when close. This is taught in Module 4.

👥 Tips for Mixed-Age Groups

  • Junior scouts (12–14): Focus on T1 and MCQ. T3 and T4 may be optional extension for this group. Pair with Senior or Open scout in the field.
  • Senior scouts (15–17): Should complete all tiers. Encourage them to explain the code tasks to juniors.
  • Open category (18+): Expected to complete all tiers including T4. May assist facilitators with equipment setup and younger scouts.
  • Mixed teams in the field: Assign roles — one person holds the antenna, one reads the compass, one marks the map. Rotate roles between foxes.
  • Language: Key terms (Yagi, attenuator, bearing, azimuth) should be introduced bilingually (English + Bahasa Melayu) for Malaysian scouts.
